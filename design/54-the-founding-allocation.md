# 54 — The founding allocation: three options

**28 September 2026. For the author to decide.** GPT's review asked who
received the Commonwealth's scarce rights in 2064, and on what terms the
public can take them back. The bible settles a great deal around the
question and not the question itself:

- volume leases are the household store of wealth: long-dated, inheritable,
  and valuable because of position, which is unearned in Henry George's sense
  (§7.5.2);
- the Freehold Party is the landlord class, and anti-Georgist (§8.5);
- the thermal quota is traded, and its price is set by an appropriation vote
  (§7.5.2);
- the Perigee Charter of 2064 bought the consortiums and the engineering
  guilds' consent with permanent functional seats (§4.6).

Nothing says who held the volume on Charter Day, or on what tenure. Each
option below is consistent with all four facts. They differ in who the
landlord class is, and in what a government can do about it.

## The thermal quota, in all three

Heat rejection is the one right no option needs to hand out. The bible
already says that how many people can exist is a public works
appropriation. So in every option the Commonwealth issues thermal quota
each year, by appropriation, and it can be traded but not owned outright.
The fight over heat is annual and budgetary. The fight over volume is the
one that goes back to 2064.

## Option A: the builders' grant

The consortiums that financed and built the stations between 2058 and 2064
were paid in volume. The Charter confirmed their long leases as property,
with a takings clause: the public can recover a lease only by buying it at
assessed value.

- **The landlord class** is the consortiums, and the families who bought
  leases from them in the first decade. The Freehold Party speaks for both.
- **What a government can do:** tax the leases' rental value, or buy leases
  back at a price the Treasury cannot afford.
- **For the story:** a Georgist government fights the Charter itself. It is
  the most fixed of the three options, and it is the one that most rewards
  the consortiums for having built the Commonwealth.

## Option B: the settlers' leases

The Charter granted every household resident on Charter Day a long,
inheritable lease on the berth it occupied, free. Everyone who arrived later
rents: from those households, or from a small public stock.

- **The landlord class** is the Charter families, a large and popular group.
  Most of today's 7,086,000 arrived after 2064, and so did nearly every
  emulated mind, so tenure divides first-comers from newcomers as much as
  rich from poor.
- **What a government can do:** tax the leases, which is taxing the
  Commonwealth's founders, or wait for leases to expire generations from
  now.
- **For the story:** it gives the "nativism, inverted" of §10.2 a material
  base, and it makes every volume bill a fight about who counts as a
  founder.

## Option C: the public freehold, leased (recommended)

The Charter vested all volume in the Commonwealth, which leases it for
fixed terms. To pay for construction, the first leases were sold cheaply at
the founding auctions of 2064 to 2070, on terms of up to 99 years. Leases
sold since have cost more. The Commonwealth may decline to renew a lease at
expiry, and must compensate only for improvements.

- **The landlord class** is whoever bought cheaply at the founding
  auctions, consortiums and early families alike. They are inheritable
  leaseholders whose leases are worth far more than they paid.
- **What a government can do:** levy the rental value (the volume levy is
  then close to a true land-value tax, and a levy on a lease's value falls
  on the leaseholder, not the resident), set renewal terms, or simply not
  renew.
- **For the story:** the first large block of founding leases can fall due
  in a later campaign. That gives the Commonwealth a built-in crisis with a
  date, and makes renewal terms the Single Tax Party's great cause.

**Why C.**
- **Precedent.** Hong Kong and Canberra run on public freehold leased for
  fixed terms. Canberra is a capital built this way, much as the Winter
  Garden was.
- **Reclaiming without a takings fight.** The public can take the rights
  back by declining to renew.
- **Tax incidence.** It answers GPT's point: a levy on a fixed, publicly
  created location falls on the holder of its rent.

**What changes if C is chosen:**
- **The bible:** §7.5.2 gains the tenure, §11.1 gains the founding auctions
  (2064 to 2070), and §7.3 is corrected. At present it says every levy
  "passes into the price of what it taxes". Under C the volume levy falls
  mostly on the leaseholder.
- **The engine (a later Codex brief):** that correction becomes a change to
  the volume levy's pass-through in `setup.priceRules`.
